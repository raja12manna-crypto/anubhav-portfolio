Case study · Anarix.ai · 2024 – 2026

Designing a product that AI runs — and people still trust.

Two years as product designer at Anarix.ai, an AI-native B2B SaaS company for brands that sell on large online marketplaces. The company's direction was clear: move from a dashboard with AI features to a product that AI operates. My job was to make that shift feel safe, legible and fast for the people using it.

01 · The context

Busy experts, dense data, and an AI that can act.

The people using the product manage brands across marketplaces — advertising, catalogue and performance — usually under time pressure, and usually in data-heavy screens. For them, a mistake isn't a typo; it's money spent in the wrong place.

As AI moved from answering questions to taking actions, the design question changed too. It was no longer "how do we show the AI's output?" but "how does a manager stay in control of a system that can change their business while they're looking at something else?"

02 · The design problems

Three problems I kept coming back to.

PROBLEM 01

From answers to actions

When AI can change something live, the interface has to turn every proposal into something a person can review, adjust and approve before it runs.

Make the AI's intent reviewable before it becomes an action.
PROBLEM 02

Complexity into confidence

Enterprise analytics for marketplace advertising carries far more data than anyone can read. The design had to surface what matters and keep the rest one step away.

Lead with the decision a manager needs to make, not the data available.
PROBLEM 03

One system for a moving product

A product being rebuilt around AI changes every sprint. Without shared foundations, every new AI surface would have looked and behaved differently.

Build and own a Figma design system the whole team could design and ship from.
03 · Principles

How I designed AI people could rely on.

  • 01
    AI proposes, people decide.Anything that changes money, budgets or live listings waits for a human yes — with a clear way to adjust it first.
  • 02
    Show the why, not just the what.Every recommendation carries the reason behind it, so a manager can judge it in seconds instead of trusting blindly.
  • 03
    Edit, don't restart.When the AI gets close but not right, the user refines its draft rather than throwing it away and doing it manually.
  • 04
    Design the failure states first.Low confidence, missing data, slow responses and wrong answers are designed as carefully as the happy path — that's where trust is won or lost.
  • 05
    One language for every AI surface.The same patterns for AI suggestions, status and review across the product, held together by the design system.
04 · How I worked

End to end, with the people who'd use it.

I designed across the full cycle — framing problems with product and engineering, mapping flows, prototyping in Figma, and running usability testing with users to check that AI-driven flows were understood before they shipped. Alongside the product work I built and maintained the design system that the AI surfaces were assembled from.

05 · Reflection

What this taught me about AI products.

The hardest part of designing AI isn't the clever moment when it works — it's every moment around it: the waiting, the doubt, the correction, the undo. Products that AI runs need more design for human control, not less. That's the thinking I bring to any team building with AI now.
